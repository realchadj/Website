To reconstitute a peptide, let the vial and bacteriostatic water reach room temperature, swab both stoppers with alcohol, draw a measured volume of water, and run it slowly down the inside wall of the peptide vial. Swirl gently until clear; never shake. Concentration equals peptide mass divided by water volume. Label the vial and refrigerate it at 2–8 °C.

What you need

  • The lyophilized peptide vial. The dry, freeze-dried cake as shipped. Certificates of analysis are published in the COA portal; check it and the product page for net content and storage notes before you start.
  • Bacteriostatic water. Sterile water with 0.9% benzyl alcohol. It is the standard diluent for a vial that will be entered more than once. Plain sterile water suits single-use preparation only. See choosing a diluent.
  • Sterile syringes. One to transfer the diluent (a 3 mL syringe makes 1–3 mL transfers easy to read). A fine-gauge U-100 insulin-type syringe measures small aliquot volumes afterward. Use a new syringe for every entry into a vial.
  • 70% isopropyl alcohol swabs. One fresh swab per stopper, per entry.
  • A label and a fine-tip marker. You will record the compound, lot, date, diluent volume and concentration.
  • A sharps container for used needles.

Step-by-step: how to reconstitute a lyophilized peptide

  1. Equilibrate the vials. Take the peptide vial and the bacteriostatic water out of cold storage and let both reach room temperature, usually 15–30 minutes. A cold vial collects condensation, and room-temperature diluent dissolves the cake faster.
  2. Inspect before you open anything. Confirm the label, an intact cap and crimp seal, and a dry white-to-off-white cake (GHK-Cu is blue). A cake collapsed into a sticky film may have been exposed to moisture.
  3. Sanitize both stoppers. Flip off the plastic caps. Wipe both rubber stoppers with a fresh alcohol swab and let them air-dry for about 30 seconds. The stoppers are the only surfaces the needle passes through, so they must stay aseptic.
  4. Decide the diluent volume. The volume of water sets the concentration: mass in the vial divided by volume added. Work the number out before you mix, because you cannot easily take water back out. The reconstitution calculator gives the concentration and per-aliquot volumes for any combination, and how much water to add explains how to choose.
  5. Draw the diluent. With a new sterile syringe, pull back the plunger to the volume you plan to draw and push that air into the bacteriostatic water vial first. This equalizes pressure and makes the draw smooth. Invert the vial, draw slightly past your mark, tap out any bubbles, and push back to the exact volume.
  6. Add the water slowly, down the vial wall. Insert the needle through the center of the peptide vial's stopper and angle it so the tip touches the inside wall of the glass. Depress the plunger slowly so the water runs down the wall and reaches the cake from below. Never jet the stream straight onto the powder. Force and turbulence cause foaming and can shear fragile peptide chains. Many lyophilized vials are sealed under partial vacuum, so the vial may pull the plunger in on its own. Hold the plunger and control the rate.
  7. Swirl gently until clear. Never shake. Withdraw the needle and rotate or roll the vial slowly between your fingers. Most peptides dissolve within one to five minutes. Larger or more hydrophobic sequences can take 10–15 minutes. Letting the vial stand between gentle swirls works better than agitating it. The finished solution should be clear and free of particles.
  8. Label and store. Write the compound, lot number, date, diluent and volume, and the resulting concentration on the vial. Refrigerate at 2–8 °C, protected from light. Bacteriostatic-water preparations are conventionally held up to about 28 days. Sterile-water preparations are used promptly. Details are in storage.

If the solution stays cloudy, foams, or leaves powder on the stopper, go to troubleshooting before you do anything else to the vial.

How to choose the volume

Work through these five checks. Most labs land on 1–3 mL for a 5–10 mg vial.

  1. Measurability. On a U-100 syringe, an aliquot under about 2 units (0.02 mL) measures poorly; 5 units or more is comfortable. If an aliquot comes out too small, add more water so the same amount occupies a larger volume.
  2. Vial capacity. A typical peptide vial holds about 3 mL of diluent at most.
  3. Round numbers. Pick a volume that makes the concentration easy to work with. 2 mL into a 10 mg vial gives 5 mg/mL, which is 50 mcg per unit. 3 mL gives 3.33 mg/mL, which invites rounding errors.
  4. Solubility. Every peptide has a concentration limit. A vial that clears at 3 mL may stay hazy at 1 mL.
  5. How fast the vial will be used. A bacteriostatic preparation has a conventional window of about 28 days, so size the volume to what will be used within it.

Concentration math, worked

Three conversions cover almost every reconstitution calculation.

  • Concentration (mg/mL) = vial mass (mg) ÷ diluent volume (mL).
  • mcg per mL = mg/mL × 1,000. 1 mg is 1,000 mcg.
  • mcg per U-100 unit = mg/mL × 10. A U-100 syringe holds 100 units per mL, so 1 unit is 0.01 mL. Multiply mcg/mL by 0.01 and you get mg/mL × 10.

To find the volume that holds a given amount, divide the amount by the concentration. For example, 500 mcg from a 5 mg/mL solution is 500 ÷ 5,000 = 0.1 mL, which is 10 units on a U-100 syringe.

Choosing a diluent

Bacteriostatic water

Bacteriostatic water is sterile water containing 0.9% benzyl alcohol. The benzyl alcohol does not sterilize anything. It suppresses bacterial growth after the stopper has been punctured, which is why it is the standard choice for a vial that will be entered repeatedly. Most research peptides dissolve in it readily. Our guide to bac water for peptides covers grades, storage and what to look for on the label.

Sterile water

Sterile water dissolves peptides exactly as bacteriostatic water does, but it contains no preservative. Once punctured, it offers no protection against contamination. Use it for a single-use preparation, or for work where benzyl alcohol would interfere with the assay, and use the solution promptly.

Dilute acetic acid and DMSO for poorly soluble peptides

A small number of peptides will not dissolve well in water at a useful concentration. Solubility depends on the sequence. Peptides with many basic residues (lysine, arginine, histidine) tend to dissolve better at lower pH. Peptides with many hydrophobic residues may need an organic solvent. Two established laboratory approaches:

  • Dilute acetic acid for basic, positively charged peptides. The peptide is dissolved in a small volume of dilute acetic acid and then brought to final volume with water or buffer.
  • DMSO for uncharged, hydrophobic peptides. The peptide is dissolved in the minimum volume of DMSO, then diluted stepwise with the aqueous diluent. DMSO can oxidize cysteine and methionine side chains, and many cell-based assays tolerate only about 1% DMSO in the final solution, so it is not a default choice.

These are specialist methods for specific sequences, not general steps. Use them only when the product page or a published method for that compound calls for them, and test solubility on a small portion first. None of the compounds below need them in routine preparation.

What not to use

Tap, bottled or non-sterile distilled water introduces microbial and endotoxin contamination. Saline and dextrose are not standard peptide diluents.

Compound-specific handling notes

These notes cover handling and solubility only. Each product page states the storage conditions for that compound.

  • BPC-157. A 15-amino-acid peptide that dissolves readily in bacteriostatic water. It usually clears within a minute or two. The standard procedure above applies unchanged. For structure and stability background, see the BPC-157 research overview.
  • Retatrutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide. Longer peptides carrying a fatty-acid side chain. They can take a little longer to clear than short peptides, so allow the vial to stand between gentle swirls rather than working it harder. The vial sizes are larger (10–30 mg is common), so check the capacity limit before choosing a volume. Vial-specific math is on the retatrutide calculator and the semaglutide calculator.
  • GHK-Cu. A copper-peptide complex, and its solution is blue. That color is expected and is a useful first check: a pale or colorless solution suggests missing or uncomplexed copper. Copper complexes are sensitive to conditions that can displace the metal, so protect the solution from prolonged light and heat. The chemistry is explained in why GHK-Cu turns blue.

Reconstituting GLOW, KLOW and other blend vials

A blend vial contains several compounds lyophilized together in one cake. GLOW (BPC-157, TB-500 and GHK-Cu) and KLOW (the same three plus KPV) are the common examples. BPC-157 + TB-500 and retatrutide + tirzepatide work the same way.

The label mass of a blend (a 70 mg GLOW vial, an 80 mg KLOW vial) is the combined nominal mass of all components. The procedure and the math are the same as for a single-compound vial. Divide the total mass by the diluent volume for the total concentration. Then multiply by each component's share to get its concentration. Those shares are fixed. Adding more or less water changes every component's concentration together; it cannot change one on its own.

For example, the current 80 mg KLOW vial contains 50 mg GHK-Cu and 10 mg each of BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV. At 4 mL, that is 20 mg/mL total, 12.5 mg/mL GHK-Cu and 2.5 mg/mL of each other component. The full breakdown by volume is in KLOW reconstitution: concentration math.

Blends are prepared exactly as above: diluent down the vial wall, gentle swirling. The multi-component cake may take a few extra minutes to clear. GHK-Cu blends should come out blue.

Storing peptides before and after reconstitution

Before reconstitution (lyophilized powder)

The dry form is the stable form. Keep sealed vials protected from light and moisture. Freezing at −20 °C is standard for long-term holding. Refrigeration at 2–8 °C is fine for material you plan to use in the near term. Lyophilized peptides are routinely shipped at ambient temperature; move them to cold storage on arrival. Reconstitute when you need the material, not in advance.

After reconstitution (solution)

Refrigerate at 2–8 °C, upright and protected from light. Avoid the refrigerator door, where temperature swings every time it opens.

Preparations in bacteriostatic water are conventionally held up to about 28 days in laboratory practice. The figure comes from pharmacy practice: USP General Chapter <797> gives opened multiple-dose containers a 28-day limit unless the manufacturer states otherwise. It is a sterility convention, not a stability guarantee for any particular peptide. Some compounds degrade faster in solution, so follow the product page if it says otherwise. Sterile-water preparations have no preservative and are used promptly.

Freezing a reconstituted solution

Avoid freeze–thaw cycles, which degrade peptides. Never refreeze a thawed solution. If a solution must be held longer than its refrigerated window, research labs divide it into single-use aliquots in sterile tubes and freeze those once at −20 °C or colder. Each aliquot is thawed once and used. Freezing the whole multi-use vial and thawing it repeatedly is the practice to avoid.

Signs a solution should be discarded

Discard a solution from research use if it turns cloudy after having been clear, develops particles or strands, changes color (or a GHK-Cu solution fades markedly), has been left warm for an extended period, or has passed its window. When in doubt, prepare a fresh vial.

Troubleshooting

The solution is cloudy or has particles

First, give it time. Let the vial stand at room temperature for 10–15 minutes with an occasional gentle swirl. Cold diluent and a dense cake both slow dissolution. If it is still hazy, the concentration may be above that peptide's solubility limit at that volume. Adding more diluent lowers the concentration and often clears it. Recalculate and relabel if you do. A solution that stays cloudy or shows particles after that points to incomplete dissolution or degraded material. Do not use it. Contact us with the lot number.

It foamed

Foam comes from agitation or from jetting the water onto the cake. Stop swirling and let the vial stand upright, refrigerated once dissolved, until the bubbles settle, which can take an hour or more. Foam alone does not ruin a vial, but aliquots drawn through foam read the wrong volume.

Powder is stuck to the stopper or the vial wall

Powder often ends up on the underside of the stopper or up the glass during shipping. After adding the diluent, tilt the vial and roll it slowly so the liquid washes over the stuck powder, then stand it upright and wait. Repeat a few times. Do not shake it and do not invert it hard. Material left undissolved on the stopper is not in solution, so the real concentration will be lower than your calculation.

The vial pulled the water in on its own, or the plunger fights back

Many lyophilized vials are sealed under partial vacuum. When the needle goes in, the vacuum draws the diluent in quickly. Keep a finger on the plunger so the water still runs down the wall slowly. The opposite problem, a plunger that pushes back, means pressure has built up after adding the diluent. Before withdrawing the needle, lift its tip above the liquid (still inside the stopper) and let the plunger settle so the pressure equalizes. For later aliquot draws, injecting an equal volume of air first keeps the pressure balanced.

Common math errors

Bad preparations nearly always come from arithmetic, not technique. These are the ones that matter.

  • Confusing mg with mcg. 1 mg = 1,000 mcg. Mixing them up is a thousandfold error. Write the unit next to every number.
  • Reading syringe "units" as an amount of peptide. On a U-100 syringe, units are volume markings: 1 unit = 0.01 mL. The peptide per unit depends on the concentration.
  • Using the wrong syringe scale. U-100 syringes hold 100 units per mL. U-40 syringes hold 40 units per mL. The "mcg per unit = mg/mL × 10" shortcut applies to U-100 only.
  • Dividing by the vial's capacity instead of the diluent added. A 3 mL vial with 2 mL of water in it is at mass ÷ 2, not mass ÷ 3.
  • Treating the label mass as exact. The label is nominal. If your work needs a precise concentration, check measured net content in the COA portal; how to read a certificate of analysis explains the figures.
  • Forgetting a blend is a total. An 80 mg blend is not 80 mg of each component.
  • Not relabeling after adding more water. If you dilute further to clear a hazy solution, the concentration changes. Update the label immediately.

Each error is broken down with the correct arithmetic in the common reconstitution errors section of the calculator page.

FAQ

How much bacteriostatic water do I add to a peptide vial?

There is no single correct volume. The volume sets the concentration, and the right concentration is one that makes each aliquot easy to measure. Common laboratory practice for 5–10 mg research vials is 1–3 mL. For example, 2 mL into a 5 mg vial gives 2.5 mg/mL, and 2 mL into a 10 mg vial gives 5 mg/mL. Work out the numbers with the reconstitution calculator before you mix.

How long do reconstituted peptides last in the fridge?

Preparations in bacteriostatic water, stored at 2–8 °C and protected from light, are conventionally held up to about 28 days in laboratory practice. That figure is a sterility convention for multi-use vials, not a stability guarantee, and some compounds degrade faster. Preparations in plain sterile water have no preservative and are used promptly.

Do peptides need to be refrigerated before reconstitution?

Lyophilized powder is far more stable than a solution, and it tolerates normal shipping at ambient temperature. For storage, keep sealed vials cold and dry: 2–8 °C for near-term use, or −20 °C for long-term holding. Protect them from light and moisture, and let a cold vial reach room temperature before opening it.

How long can reconstituted peptides stay at room temperature?

There is no general figure that applies to every peptide, so treat room temperature as working time only. Take the vial out, draw what is needed, and return it to 2–8 °C promptly. A solution left warm for an extended period, or one that has turned cloudy, is discarded from research use.

Can you freeze reconstituted peptides?

Avoid freezing and thawing the same vial repeatedly, because freeze–thaw cycles degrade peptides. If a solution must be held longer than its refrigerated window, research labs split it into single-use aliquots, freeze each one once at −20 °C or colder, and never refreeze a thawed aliquot.

Is reconstitution solution the same as bacteriostatic water?

Not always. "Reconstitution solution" is a general term for any liquid used to dissolve a lyophilized product, so check the label. Bacteriostatic water is sterile water with 0.9% benzyl alcohol. Sterile water has no preservative. Heartland Bio Labs' reconstitution solution is bacteriostatic water.

Can sterile water be used instead of bacteriostatic water?

For a single-use preparation, yes. Sterile water dissolves peptides the same way. It contains no antimicrobial agent, so a vial that will be entered more than once should be prepared with bacteriostatic water instead. Saline and dextrose solutions are not standard peptide diluents.

How long does a peptide take to dissolve after adding the water?

Most lyophilized peptides clear within one to five minutes of gentle swirling at room temperature. Larger or more hydrophobic sequences can take 10–15 minutes. Letting the vial stand between gentle swirls works better than agitating it. A solution that stays cloudy or shows particles after that is not used in research work.

Does the water go into the peptide vial, or the peptide into the water?

The diluent is always drawn first and added to the peptide vial, slowly and down the inside wall rather than onto the powder. Transferring lyophilized powder into a water vial risks losing material and is not standard practice.
