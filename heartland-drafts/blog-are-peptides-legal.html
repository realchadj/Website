Short answer: In the US it is generally legal to buy and sell peptides for laboratory research, and the common research peptides are not controlled substances. It is not legal to market or sell an unapproved peptide for human use. FDA decides that by a product's intended use, not by its "research use only" label. Approved peptide drugs need a prescription.

That is the rule. The details changed a lot in 2025 and 2026: new FDA warning letters, a compounding reversal on BPC-157 and eleven other peptides, an advisory committee vote, and a 70-month federal prison sentence for a peptide seller. This guide covers where things stand as of September 2026, with the primary sources linked.

What "peptide" means legally

US law has no category called "peptide." A peptide is a chain of amino acids, and the law treats it by what it is sold for. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), a "drug" includes any article "intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease" and any article (other than food) "intended to affect the structure or any function of the body" (21 U.S.C. 321(g)(1)).

So the same molecule can be a prescription drug, an unapproved drug or a laboratory reagent. The deciding question is intended use.

Many peptides are FDA-approved drugs. Insulin is a peptide. So are semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound; FDA approval, November 2023), liraglutide, tesamorelin and bremelanotide (PT-141, sold as Vyleesi). You can check any product's approval status in Drugs@FDA. Many other peptides sold online, such as BPC-157, TB-500, retatrutide and cagrilintide, are not approved for any use.

Prescription peptides vs research peptides

There are three legal channels.

  • Approved drugs. Made under an approved application, sold with FDA-approved labeling and dispensed on prescription.
  • Compounded drugs. Made by a state-licensed pharmacy (section 503A) or a registered outsourcing facility (section 503B) for patients. Compounding has its own rules about which ingredients may be used. More on that below.
  • Research materials. Sold to laboratories for experiments, not for use in people. This is where "research use only" (RUO) peptides sit.

The trouble starts when a product in the third channel is sold as if it belonged in the first. FDA says so directly: it "has warned companies that have illegally sold unapproved drugs containing semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, survodutide or mazdutide that are falsely labeled 'for research purposes' or 'not for human consumption.' These products have been sold directly to consumers for human use with dosing instructions" (FDA, updated September 1, 2026).

What "research use only" means, and its limits

Many people assume "research use only" is a legal status that FDA grants. It isn't, at least not for peptides.

The formal RUO label in FDA regulations comes from the rules for in vitro diagnostic products. It requires diagnostic products still in the research phase to carry the statement "For Research Use Only. Not for use in diagnostic procedures" (21 CFR 809.10(c)(2)(i)). No parallel regulation turns a research peptide into a lawful non-drug because of its label.

What matters is intended use, and FDA judges it objectively. Under 21 CFR 201.128, intent "may be shown by such persons' expressions, the design or composition of the article, or by the circumstances surrounding the distribution of the article," including "labeling claims, advertising matter, or oral or written statements."

In practice, FDA reads the whole website, not just the vial. A disclaimer does not help a seller whose other words point to people.

An RUO label is a necessary part of selling for research. It is not enough on its own. A seller's claims, product pages, bundles, social posts and customer service all count.

What FDA actually enforces

FDA warning letters show how the agency reads RUO websites. Two examples:

March 31, 2026: an RUO peptide seller

FDA warned a California-based online seller of retatrutide, tirzepatide and bacteriostatic water (warning letter 721806). The letter says: "Despite statements on your product labeling marketing your products for 'Research Use Only,' and 'not intended for human consumption, medical use, or veterinary use,' evidence obtained from your website establishes that your products are intended to be drugs for human use."

The evidence FDA quoted was not dosing charts. It was research-flavored copy about appetite, glucose, body weight and lipids, including a "Benefits" list describing a compound as "studied for its ability to lower glucose." FDA also treated the bacteriostatic water as a drug: selling it next to peptides that need reconstitution "demonstrates that you intend for your 'Bacteriostatic Water for Injection' to be used in combination for injection." The products were cited as unapproved new drugs under 21 U.S.C. 355(a).

September 9, 2025: compounded retatrutide

FDA warned a telehealth seller offering "compounded" retatrutide (warning letter 713650). Retatrutide cannot be compounded legally, because it has no USP monograph, is not part of an approved drug and is not on the 503A bulks list. FDA now states plainly that "retatrutide and cagrilintide cannot be used in compounding under federal law" (FDA).

Beyond warning letters

Enforcement has moved past letters. On July 30, 2026, the owner of a peptide company was sentenced to 70 months in federal prison after pleading guilty to introducing unapproved new drugs into interstate commerce with intent to defraud or mislead, and to illegal importation. Prosecutors said the business falsely claimed its products were tested, US-made and FDA-approved. The court entered a $5 million money judgment (US Attorney, N.D. Indiana).

The same statute also covers buyers. The FD&C Act prohibits "the receipt in interstate commerce" of a misbranded drug (21 U.S.C. 331(c)). Enforcement has focused on sellers, but "I only bought it" is not a complete legal answer when the product was sold for human use.

Compounding and peptides

Compounding is how many people get peptides with a prescription. It is also where most of the 2026 news happened.

The 503A bulks list and FDA's categories

A 503A pharmacy may compound from a bulk ingredient only if it has a USP or NF monograph, is a component of an approved drug, or appears on FDA's 503A bulks list (21 U.S.C. 353a(b)(1)(A)(i)). While that list is built, FDA sorts nominated substances into interim categories. Category 1 substances may be compounded under FDA's enforcement discretion while they are evaluated. Category 2 holds substances FDA says "may present significant safety risks."

In 2023 FDA placed many popular peptides in Category 2. In April 2026 their nominators withdrew those nominations, and FDA moved them to a "nominated but withdrawn" list (FDA Category 2 page, updated April 22, 2026). That list includes BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, Selank, Epitalon, DSIP (emideltide), GHK-Cu for injection, AOD-9604, CJC-1295, thymosin alpha-1, LL-37, Dihexa, PEG-MGF and Melanotan II. FDA's safety concerns about each one still appear on that page.

Leaving Category 2 did not, by itself, put these peptides in Category 1 or on the bulks list. FDA's interim policy covers only substances that were nominated with adequate support and not flagged for safety risks.

Some peptides remain in Category 2 today. Kisspeptin-10 is in 503A Category 2. Ipamorelin acetate, GHRP-2 and GHRP-6 are in 503B Category 2, and ibutamoren (MK-677) is in both.

The July 2026 advisory committee

On July 23 and 24, 2026, FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee reviewed seven peptides for the 503A bulks list: BPC-157 (reviewed for ulcerative colitis), KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, emideltide (DSIP), Semax and Epitalon (FDA meeting page and webcast). The committee voted in favor of six and against emideltide.

Advisory committees "make non-binding recommendations to the FDA," as FDA's meeting page puts it. A substance joins the 503A bulks list through notice-and-comment rulemaking under 21 CFR 216.23. At the time of this review, FDA had not finalized that step. Even when it does, a listed peptide is still not an FDA-approved drug. It can only be compounded by a pharmacy for a patient with a prescription. Listing does nothing for research-labeled products sold to consumers.

The GLP-1 shortage ended

Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide spread because both drugs were on FDA's shortage list, which lets compounders make copies. FDA declared the tirzepatide shortage resolved on December 19, 2024, and the semaglutide shortage resolved on February 21, 2025. The grace periods for 503A pharmacies ended in March 2025 (tirzepatide) and April 2025 (semaglutide). Outsourcing facilities had until March 19 and May 22, 2025 (FDA).

Today a 503A pharmacy cannot "regularly or in inordinate amounts" compound copies of these approved drugs. FDA says it does not intend to act against four or fewer such prescriptions a month. Adding an ingredient such as B12 does not by itself make a compounded drug different enough (FDA update, April 1, 2026). On April 30, 2026, FDA proposed keeping semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide off the 503B bulks list, finding "no clinical need" (FDA; Federal Register). The comment period was extended to July 30, 2026 (Federal Register).

Are any peptides controlled substances?

The research peptides discussed here are not on the DEA's controlled substance schedules (21 CFR part 1308). Anabolic steroids such as testosterone are Schedule III (21 CFR 1308.13). That matters because a product mislabeled as a peptide or SARM that actually contains a steroid is a different legal problem.

Human growth hormone has its own criminal statute. It is a federal felony to knowingly distribute, or possess with intent to distribute, hGH "for any use in humans other than the treatment of a disease or other recognized medical condition" authorized by FDA and "pursuant to the order of a physician" (21 U.S.C. 333(e)). The statute defines hGH as "somatrem, somatropin, or an analogue of either of them," and the DEA may investigate. Growth hormone secretagogues such as sermorelin, CJC-1295 and ipamorelin are different molecules that act upstream of hGH. They are regulated as drugs under the general FD&C Act rules above. Peptides and sports

Sport has stricter rules than federal law. The World Anti-Doping Agency's 2026 Prohibited List bans many peptides at all times (WADA Prohibited List):

  • S0, Non-approved substances: any substance with no current approval for human therapeutic use. The list names BPC-157 as an example. It also covers investigational drugs such as retatrutide.
  • S2, Peptide hormones and growth factors: GHRH analogues (CJC-1295, sermorelin, tesamorelin), growth hormone secretagogues (ipamorelin, ibutamoren), GHRPs (GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin), growth hormone fragments (AOD-9604), IGF-1 and its analogues, mechano growth factors, and "thymosin-β4 and its derivatives e.g. TB-500."

Leagues and bodies that follow WADA rules, and many that write their own, treat these as violations whether or not the product was legal to buy.

A note on state law

States regulate pharmacies, medical practice and consumer protection. They have used those powers against peptide sellers. In February 2025, a coalition of 38 state and territory attorneys general asked FDA to act against online retailers selling GLP-1 ingredients labeled "for research purposes only" or "not for human consumption" while advertising them to consumers (NAAG). Connecticut's attorney general sued and settled with a seller of "bootleg" GLP-1s and pursued a platform over "research grade" GLP-1 sales (Connecticut AG).

State rules differ and change. Check your own state's pharmacy board and attorney general guidance.

How to tell a supplier who respects the law from one who doesn't

The warning letters and the 2026 criminal case point to the same red flags:

  • Human-use language anywhere. Dosing, "protocols," injection tips, benefit lists, before-and-after stories. FDA quotes these back in warning letters, even when they sit beside an RUO disclaimer.
  • Claims tied to approved drugs. "Same as Ozempic," "generic Mounjaro" or "FDA-approved" on an unapproved product.
  • Documentation you can't check. The criminal case involved claims of testing and US manufacturing that were false. A certificate of analysis should name the lot, the method and the lab, and you should be able to verify it.
  • No purchaser terms. A research supplier should state who may buy and what the material may not be used for.

FAQ

Are peptides legal in the US?

Yes, with limits. Many peptides are FDA-approved prescription drugs. Unapproved peptides can generally be bought and sold for laboratory research. It is illegal to market or sell an unapproved peptide for human use, and FDA judges that by intended use, not by the label.

Are peptides illegal?

Peptides as a class are not illegal, and the common research peptides are not DEA-controlled substances. What is illegal is selling an unapproved peptide as a drug for people, compounding it from an ineligible ingredient, or distributing human growth hormone for non-medical use.

Is BPC-157 legal?

BPC-157 is not FDA-approved for any use. It can be sold for laboratory research. In April 2026 it left FDA's Category 2 compounding list, and in July 2026 an FDA advisory committee voted to recommend adding it to the 503A bulks list. That vote is not binding, and pharmacy compounding depends on FDA rulemaking. WADA prohibits BPC-157 in sport.

Is it legal to buy peptides online?

Buying peptides for genuine laboratory research is generally legal. Buying an unapproved peptide that is sold for human use is not a safe harbor. The FD&C Act prohibits receiving misbranded drugs in interstate commerce, and FDA warns these products are of unknown quality.

Is it legal to sell peptides for research?

Yes, if they are actually sold for research. A "research use only" label does not protect a seller whose website, claims or product bundles show the products are meant for people. FDA has issued warning letters on exactly that basis.

Do you need a prescription for peptides?

For approved peptide drugs such as semaglutide, tirzepatide and tesamorelin, yes. Compounded peptides also require a prescription. Research peptides are not sold for human use, so a prescription does not apply to them.

Are peptides FDA approved?

Some are. Insulin, semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide, tesamorelin and bremelanotide are FDA-approved peptide drugs. BPC-157, TB-500, retatrutide, cagrilintide and most other research peptides are not approved for any use. You can check any product in Drugs@FDA.

Are peptides legal in sports?

Many are banned. WADA's 2026 Prohibited List bans non-approved substances such as BPC-157 at all times, and bans growth hormone secretagogues, GHRH analogues, IGF-1 analogues and TB-500 under its peptide hormones section.

This is not legal advice. It is a general summary of federal law and FDA policy for readers in the United States. The rules change and depend on facts. If you make, sell, compound or prescribe peptides, talk to a lawyer who practices FDA law.
