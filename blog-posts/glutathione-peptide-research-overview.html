Glutathione is the smallest peptide in the catalog and one of the most unusual. It is only three residues long, the cell does not build it on the ribosome, and one of its peptide bonds is not a normal peptide bond at all. This overview covers what glutathione is, why its structure matters, how the reduced and oxidized forms differ, and how to handle and verify a molecule that reacts readily with air.

What Glutathione Is

Glutathione (GSH) is the tripeptide γ-L-glutamyl-L-cysteinyl-glycine, with a molecular weight of about 307 Da. It is found in almost every cell, often at millimolar concentrations, which makes it the most abundant low molecular weight thiol in most tissues. Its free cysteine thiol (-SH) group is what drives nearly all of its chemistry.

The Gamma Bond

In an ordinary peptide, each amino acid links to the next through the carboxyl group on its backbone alpha carbon. Glutathione breaks that rule: glutamate joins cysteine through the carboxyl group on the end of its side chain, the gamma position. This isopeptide bond is not recognized by most peptidases, which helps explain why glutathione persists at such high concentrations inside cells. It also explains why cells make it with two dedicated enzymes, glutamate-cysteine ligase and glutathione synthetase, rather than on the ribosome.

The GSH to GSSG ratio is one of the most widely used measures of cellular redox state in research. That makes the form and purity of the starting material especially important: a reagent that has partly oxidized in the vial shifts the baseline of the whole experiment. Stability and Handling

The free thiol that makes glutathione useful also makes it unstable in solution. Exposure to oxygen, trace metal ions, and neutral to alkaline pH all speed its oxidation to GSSG. As a dry powder it is considerably more stable.

  • Store the lyophilized material cold, dry, and tightly sealed.
  • Let the vial reach room temperature before opening to prevent condensation.
  • Prepare solutions fresh where possible, keep them cold, and limit headspace air in the vial.
  • Aliquot any solution that must be kept, and avoid repeated freeze thaw cycles.

Quality Verification

For glutathione, the key quality question is not just purity but the proportion of reduced to oxidized form. HPLC separates GSH from GSSG and quantifies each. Mass spectrometry confirms identity, with GSSG appearing at roughly double the monomer mass. Thiol-specific assays such as Ellman's reagent (DTNB) are also commonly used in the lab to measure free thiol content directly. Certificates are published through the COA portal, and our guide to HPLC versus mass spectrometry explains how the two methods complement each other.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is glutathione a peptide?

Yes. Glutathione is a tripeptide of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, although the glutamate is linked through its side chain gamma carboxyl rather than the usual backbone bond.

What is the difference between GSH and GSSG?

GSH is reduced glutathione, a single molecule with a free thiol. GSSG is oxidized glutathione, two molecules joined by a disulfide bond.

Why does glutathione oxidize in solution?

Its cysteine thiol reacts with dissolved oxygen, a reaction accelerated by trace metals and higher pH. The dry powder is far more stable than any solution.

How is glutathione purity measured?

HPLC quantifies purity and separates the reduced and oxidized forms, and mass spectrometry confirms identity.
