Oxytocin holds a unique place in peptide chemistry: it was the first peptide hormone ever synthesized in a laboratory, and it remains one of the most studied neuropeptides in the world. This overview covers what oxytocin is structurally, its relationship to vasopressin, why it is known for poor heat stability, and how research grade material is verified.

What Oxytocin Is

Oxytocin is a nonapeptide with the sequence Cys-Tyr-Ile-Gln-Asn-Cys-Pro-Leu-Gly-NH₂ and a molecular weight of about 1,007 Da. The two cysteines at positions 1 and 6 are joined by a disulfide bond, closing the first six residues into a ring, with a three residue tail ending in an amidated glycine. In mammals it is produced in the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.

A Landmark in Peptide Chemistry

In 1953 Vincent du Vigneaud and his group determined oxytocin's structure and then synthesized it, the first time any peptide hormone had been made from scratch. The work earned du Vigneaud the 1955 Nobel Prize in Chemistry and showed that a hormone's biological activity comes entirely from its chemical structure. Much of modern peptide synthesis, including the methods used for every compound in this catalog, builds on that result.

The two hormones differ at only two positions, 3 and 8, yet act on different receptor families. That near identity makes them a classic model for studying how small sequence changes shift receptor selectivity. Some cross reactivity between the systems is well documented, which matters when designing receptor studies.

The Research Context

The oxytocin receptor (OXTR) is a G protein coupled receptor. Oxytocin research spans uterine and mammary smooth muscle physiology, where its role was first identified, and a large body of work on social behavior, bonding, and stress response in animal models. Oxytocin is also an FDA approved prescription drug for specific obstetric uses; the material supplied here is a research reagent, not a pharmaceutical product. Heat Sensitivity: Why Cold Storage Matters

Oxytocin is well known for losing potency when exposed to heat. The disulfide bond and the asparagine and glutamine residues are the weak points, prone to rearrangement and deamidation as temperature rises. The problem is serious enough that the World Health Organization recommends that oxytocin products be stored and transported under refrigeration. For research material, that means:

  • Store lyophilized oxytocin cold, dry, and dark, with -20°C preferred for long term storage.
  • Keep solutions at 2 to 8°C, protect them from light, and use them within a validated window.
  • Divide into aliquots to avoid repeated freeze thaw cycles.

Quality Verification

For a cyclic peptide, identity testing has to confirm the disulfide as well as the sequence. The closed ring is 2 Da lighter than the open, reduced form, a difference mass spectrometry can resolve. Dimers formed by bonds between two molecules show up at roughly double the mass. HPLC quantifies purity and separates deamidated degradation products. Frequently Asked Questions

Is oxytocin a peptide?

Yes. Oxytocin is a nine amino acid peptide hormone with a disulfide bond between the cysteines at positions 1 and 6.

What is the difference between oxytocin and vasopressin?

They differ at two of nine positions: isoleucine versus phenylalanine at position 3, and leucine versus arginine at position 8. Those two changes direct them to different receptor families.

Does oxytocin need to be refrigerated?

Oxytocin is heat sensitive. Cold storage is standard for both the dry powder and any solution.

Who first synthesized oxytocin?

Vincent du Vigneaud, in 1953. It was the first peptide hormone ever synthesized, and the work won the 1955 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
