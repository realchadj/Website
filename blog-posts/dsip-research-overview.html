DSIP is one of the oldest and least understood neuropeptides in research: a short, simple sequence that was discovered almost fifty years ago and still has no confirmed receptor. This overview covers what DSIP is structurally, where it came from, what makes it hard to work with, and how research grade material is verified.

What DSIP Is

Delta sleep inducing peptide (DSIP) is a nonapeptide, nine amino acids long, with the sequence Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu (WAGGDASGE) and a molecular weight of roughly 849 Da. It carries no disulfide bridge, no unusual residues, and no terminal modifications. By the standards of this catalog it is one of the simplest molecules we supply.

Where It Came From

DSIP was isolated in 1977 by a Swiss research group studying the cerebral venous blood of rabbits kept in a state of electrically induced slow wave sleep. The name reflects the context of that original work, the delta waves that dominate deep sleep recordings, rather than a settled understanding of what the peptide does. Decades of follow up studies have produced a mixed and sometimes contradictory literature, which is part of why DSIP remains an active research tool instead of a closed question.

The Missing Receptor

Most peptides in the catalog have a well characterized target. The GHRH analogues act on the GHRH receptor, and the ghrelin mimetics act on GHS-R1a. DSIP does not fit that pattern: no dedicated receptor has been identified, and proposed mechanisms range from modulation of other neurotransmitter systems to indirect effects on stress hormone signaling. For a researcher, that open question is the point. DSIP is typically studied as a probe for how a small, unmodified peptide can influence neural systems without an obvious binding partner.

Stability and Handling

The same simplicity that makes DSIP easy to synthesize makes it vulnerable. An unmodified linear peptide has no protecting groups at either terminus, so aminopeptidases in biological matrices break it down quickly. That short persistence is one reason experimental results vary so widely between studies. The tryptophan at position 1 is also the residue most sensitive to oxidation and light.

  • Store the lyophilized material cold, dry, and dark, ideally at -20°C for long term storage.
  • Let the vial reach room temperature before opening so condensation does not form on the powder.
  • Keep solutions refrigerated, protected from light, and divided into aliquots to avoid repeated freeze thaw cycles.

Quality Verification

Because DSIP is small and unmodified, the main verification risks are truncated or deletion sequences from synthesis, which differ from the target by one residue and can be hard to separate. Mass spectrometry confirms the intact nine residue mass, HPLC quantifies purity, and net peptide content ties the label to the actual amount of peptide in the vial. Frequently Asked Questions

What does DSIP stand for?

DSIP stands for delta sleep inducing peptide. The name comes from the conditions of its discovery in 1977, not from an established mechanism.

What is the DSIP amino acid sequence?

Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu, written in single letter code as WAGGDASGE.

Does DSIP have a known receptor?

No. A dedicated DSIP receptor has not been identified, and its mechanism of action remains an open research question.

How should lyophilized DSIP be stored?

Cold, dry, and protected from light, with -20°C preferred for long term storage. Reconstituted solutions should be refrigerated and aliquoted.
